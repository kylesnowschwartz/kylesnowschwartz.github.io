How to use this

Print it and take it in. The checklist in Before and at your appointment has tick boxes for the things to say and ask, which is the part that matters most on the day. Read the rest once; you do not need to remember it.

→ Start this today

Photograph the dark right big toe once a day — same spot, same light, foot relaxed and not held or pressed. The surgeon needs to see which way it is heading, not one moment. Holding a foot for the camera squeezes the blood out of the skin and creates white patches that are not really there, so let it rest first.

One caution about the photographs you already have. Phone cameras shift skin tone badly, and callus is yellow-white on its own and is not a sign of circulation. A colour change only counts when it appears again with the foot relaxed, unhandled, and in daylight.

Worth doing today

Seven things that cost little or nothing, carry almost no risk, and do not need anyone’s permission. Full detail is in What to do at home.

  1. Keep your whole body warm, starting now. The highest value thing on this page and it is free. Warm torso, warm socks and shoes indoors, dressed before you get cold rather than after.
  2. Photograph the dark toe daily, as described above.
  3. Ring the GP for the toe pressure test. Fifteen minutes, no special equipment, and it either reassures you or moves your appointment forward.
  4. Take your medicine list to any pharmacist. Free, no appointment, same day. Ask them to check whether anything you take narrows blood vessels. Decongestants and some over-the-counter remedies do, and this is exactly the kind of question a pharmacist answers on the spot.
  5. Loosen your footwear and stop cold-weather rides until you have been seen.
  6. Dig out the oncology records for the brachytherapy details, and write down your answers to the questions in Before and at your appointment.
  7. Cut caffeine back and keep off tobacco entirely.

A fair question, and the answer is ask, do not self-start — but ask this week rather than in three weeks.

If the cause turns out to be artery disease or small travelling clots, a low daily dose of aspirin is a standard treatment and a specialist would very likely start you on it. So it is not a strange idea. But aspirin causes stomach bleeding in a minority of people, it does nothing for the vessel spasm of Raynaud’s itself, and whether it suits you depends on your other medicines and your stomach history.

That is a two-minute decision for a GP and a bad one to make alone. Put it on the same phone call as the toe pressure test: “given what is going on with my feet, should I be on low-dose aspirin now?” The same applies to any supplement sold for circulation.

If things change before your appointment

Nothing here is a prediction. It is on the page for the same reason a smoke alarm is: so that if one specific, recognisable thing happens, nobody has to work out what to do while it is happening.

The reasoning is straightforward. Reduced blood supply is something you can live with for a long time. Blood supply that stops damages tissue within hours. The signs below are the ones that tell those apart.

Straight to hospital

! Go to A&E now if either of these happens

A&E is the hospital emergency department. Go the same hour, not the next day.

  • A foot goes cold and pale or blotchy and stays that way after warming up. A Raynaud’s attack looks much like this, but it settles with warmth within minutes to an hour. One that does not settle can mean an artery has blocked.
  • Sudden numbness or weakness in a foot or leg. Nerves need more blood than skin does, so they complain first when supply drops sharply. Treat it as a signal about timing, not a symptom to sit out.

Same day, but not hospital

→ Ring your GP today, or use urgent care, if
  • A toe turns black, or skin breaks open into a sore. This means a new area, or the dark right big toe getting visibly worse. A sore on a foot with reduced blood supply will not close on its own and can become infected, so getting it cleaned and dressed early is what keeps it small.
  • Pain becomes constant at rest, wakes you at night, and eases when you hang the foot out of bed. That means the foot is relying on gravity to get enough blood, and it is a clear reason to have the vascular appointment brought forward.

What to say at A&E

Emergency departments sort people by what they hear in the first thirty seconds. Saying the right things is not about sounding dramatic — it is about reaching the right team instead of the general queue. Open with something close to this:

“I think I may have a blood supply problem in my foot. It has gone cold and pale and it has not come back with warming.

I have diagnosed Raynaud’s, and my right big toe has been dark and very tender for several weeks. I had brachytherapy for prostate cancer. I already have a vascular surgery appointment booked.

Could someone check the pulses in my foot with a Doppler, and could you ask whether the vascular team should see me?”

Three parts of that carry the weight. Cold and pale, not coming back with warming separates a blocked artery from a Raynaud’s attack. A toe dark for several weeks tells them the foot already had reduced blood supply before today, so there is less margin than the current picture suggests. Already under vascular surgery tells them this is a known problem that has changed, not a new mystery.

Then ask for two things, in this order: a handheld Doppler check of the foot pulses, which is done at the bedside in two minutes, and an answer to “should the on-call vascular team see me?” Ask the second one again before you leave if nobody has answered it. The fuller list of tests is in the checklist, and the same requests apply.

Take the printed page, your daily photographs, your medicine list, and the year of the prostate treatment if you know it.

Meanwhile, this week

→ Book a routine GP appointment

This is the calm version of everything above, and the path you are far more likely to take. Ask for an ankle-brachial index — blood pressure measured at your ankle and compared with your arm — and, more importantly, a toe pressure reading. It takes about fifteen minutes and needs nothing a GP surgery does not already have.

A normal result is real reassurance for very little trouble. An abnormal one is a concrete reason to move the vascular appointment earlier, which is a much better way to be seen sooner than waiting for something to go wrong.

What is going on

Raynaud’s is small blood vessels in the fingers and toes clamping shut in response to cold or stress, so the skin goes white, often then blue, then red as blood returns. It comes in two forms, and which form this is matters more than the diagnosis itself.

Primary (Raynaud’s disease) Secondary (Raynaud’s phenomenon)
Cause None. It is the whole condition. Another disease is driving it.
Usual age at onset 15–30 Later in life, often over 40
Who Mostly women Either sex
Pattern Symmetrical, both sides alike Often uneven between limbs and toes
Over time Stable, or improves Can get worse
Tissue damage Does not cause sores or tissue loss Can cause sores and tissue loss

Your case matches none of the primary criteria and all of the secondary ones: it started at 68, in a man, unevenly between toes and between feet, and it is getting worse. So the question worth answering is not whether this is Raynaud’s. It is what is causing the Raynaud’s.

Three things Raynaud’s alone does not explain

  1. A toe that stays dark. A Raynaud’s colour change is an episode: it comes with cold or stress and goes with warmth, over minutes to hours. Your right big toe has stayed dark and painfully tender across weeks. That is not an episode — it means the blood supply to it is short continuously, and it is the finding that sets the urgency on this page.
  2. Steady worsening. Primary Raynaud’s stays put or improves. Months of getting worse without levelling off points to an underlying process that is itself progressing.
  3. Hands as well as feet. Symptoms in all four limbs point to something affecting the whole body rather than a problem confined to the leg arteries. That raises the priority of the autoimmune blood tests.
What follows from this

Never having smoked removes the smoking-related causes and makes ordinary furring of the arteries a weaker explanation in a fit man with normal blood pressure and no diabetes. When the usual culprits are absent and the symptoms are still there, an autoimmune condition or small clots travelling from higher up become the more likely explanations.

So you need two referrals, not one: vascular surgery for the arteries and the dark toe, and rheumatology for the autoimmune causes that blood tests and a nailfold examination can find.

Possible causes, ranked

Ordered by how well each fits your symptoms, not by how likely it is. None is established. They are here so the right tests get ordered.

  1. 1
    Atheroembolism, or “blue toe syndrome” Most important not to miss

    Small fragments break off a fatty deposit or a bulge in a larger artery higher up — in the abdomen, pelvis, or behind the knee — travel down, and block the tiny vessels in a toe. The signature matches your right foot exactly: one discoloured, very tender toe on a foot that is otherwise warm.

    It is first because it is the most costly to miss, and it is missed often: the pulses usually feel normal, so the foot gets called healthy. The source needs finding, because it will keep sending fragments until it is treated.

  2. 2
    Autoimmune connective tissue disease What the blood tests look for

    Scleroderma-spectrum disease, lupus, and related conditions are the classic causes of secondary Raynaud’s, and hands and feet together point this way. This is what the rheumatology referral exists to find or rule out.

    The tests are cheap and worth doing regardless. Nailfold capillaroscopy is a magnified look at the tiny vessels at the base of a nail; they look normal in primary Raynaud’s and visibly abnormal in scleroderma-spectrum disease. Alongside it go three antibody blood tests — ANA, anti-centromere, and anti-Scl-70 — which look for signs that the immune system is attacking the body’s own connective tissue.

  3. 3
    Ordinary artery disease from fatty deposits Less likely for you than for most

    Age 68 and your mother’s vascular disease keep this on the list. But the rest of your risk profile argues against it being the main story: never smoked, no diabetes, normal blood pressure, and a high level of fitness. That combination makes it a weaker explanation here than it would be in most men your age.

    It is not ruled out. Endurance athletes do get artery disease, and being fit can hide the usual warning sign — calf pain on walking — for years. Disease in the small arteries below the knee causes foot symptoms without that calf pain, which is why the toe pressure matters more than the ankle pressure: stiff arteries can make an ankle reading look falsely normal.

  4. 4
    Radiation damage to the pelvic arteries Turns on one unanswered question

    Radiation can make artery walls thicken and scar, and the narrowing shows up years or decades later. When radiotherapy is delivered as an external beam through the pelvis it passes straight through the arteries feeding the legs, and this becomes a serious candidate.

    Brachytherapy is different. It treats prostate cancer with small radioactive seeds placed inside the prostate. The dose is intense at the seeds and falls away sharply with distance, so the arteries running to the legs sit well outside the treated area. Damage to them is not what seeds usually cause.

    One thing decides this entry: brachytherapy is often combined with a course of external beam radiotherapy for men at higher risk. If you had both, this moves up the list considerably. If you had seeds only, it stays here as a background possibility. Your oncology records will say — it is not worth working out from memory.

  5. 5
    Cancer-associated Raynaud’s Uncommon

    Raynaud’s appearing for the first time in an older man is a recognised, if uncommon, sign associated with an active cancer. Given the prostate history it is worth saying out loud, and it is a reason to make sure your routine cancer follow-up is up to date.

  6. 6
    Cycling pressure on the feet and hands Adds to it, does not explain it

    50–100 km a week in stiff-soled cleated shoes causes real problems: the forefoot squeezed against a narrow toe box, pain under the ball of the foot, and a thickened, painful nerve between the toes. These explain stiffness, soreness, and the callus well. They explain colour change poorly.

    The sore hands may be a separate mechanical problem rather than more Raynaud’s. Three conditions are common in high-mileage riders:

    • Handlebar palsy. Pressure on the outer edge of the palm squashes a nerve. Numbness, tingling, and weakness in the ring and little fingers. Usually fixed by changing position.
    • Carpal tunnel compression. Pressure and a bent-back wrist irritate a different nerve. Numbness in the thumb, index, and middle fingers, often worse at night.
    • Hypothenar hammer syndrome. Repeated impact of the palm against the handlebars damages an artery inside the hand, which can then throw small clots into the fingers. This one causes genuine loss of blood supply to the fingers, and it is why the hands belong at the vascular appointment even though they seem minor next to the feet.
  7. 7
    Blood disorders One blood test settles it

    Conditions where the blood carries too many red cells or platelets cause burning, painful, discoloured hands and feet, and clotting. A full blood count answers it.

  8. 8
    External iliac artery endofibrosis Rare, and a cyclist’s condition

    A thickening of an artery in the pelvis, seen almost only in high-mileage cyclists from repeated deep hip flexion. It normally shows up as power loss or thigh pain at maximum effort rather than changes at rest, so it fits poorly — but your riding volume puts you in the group it affects, and it is easy to miss without exercise testing.

Also considered, and set aside

  • Buerger’s disease. Inflammation of the small arteries of the hands and feet, otherwise a good match. It occurs essentially only in tobacco users, so it is off the list.
  • A sudden arterial blockage. Months of gradual change while still cycling rules this out as what is happening now. It could still happen later, which is what the first section is for.
  • Diabetic foot disease. No diabetes. Still worth one HbA1c blood test, which shows average blood sugar over about three months, because undiagnosed diabetes at 68 is common.
  • Skin infection. No fever, no spreading warm redness, no discharge, and both feet are involved.
  • Gout. Usually one joint turning hot, red, and swollen over hours, not a slow change across both feet. A dark tender big toe can mimic it, so not fully off until examined.
  • Plantar fasciitis. That causes heel pain worst on the first steps of the morning, and it does not change the colour of anything.
  • Callus alone. It is there and it is part of what the photographs show, but callus does not make a whole toe progressively painful or discoloured.

Before and at your appointment

Work these out beforehand

Only you can answer these, and each one changes the diagnosis. Write the answers down and take them in. Two of them — the first and the seventh — would change the plan, and neither is currently known.

  1. Do your hands change colour when you are not cycling? Handling frozen food, a cold morning, holding a cold drink. White then blue then red is Raynaud’s. Numbness and pins-and-needles with normal colour is a squashed nerve.
  2. Which fingers? Ring and little finger together, or thumb through middle finger, points to nerve compression. Scattered or all fingers points to Raynaud’s.
  3. One hand or both, and is one worse? Clearly one-sided points to a mechanical or artery cause in that arm.
  4. Does it ease within minutes of stopping and shaking your hands out? Typical of nerve compression.
  5. Does position change the colour of your feet? Photograph a relaxed foot flat, then raised at 45° for a minute, then hanging down for a minute. Going pale when raised and flushing a slow deep red when lowered is a specific sign of poor artery supply.
  6. Pain at rest or only on walking and riding, and how far can you walk before it hurts? A rough figure in metres or minutes is more useful than “a while”.
  7. Does the dark toe change with warmth, or stay the same colour all the time? A toe that lightens when warm is behaving like a severe Raynaud’s episode. One that stays the same regardless of temperature means the blood supply is continuously short, which is the more serious reading.
  8. Was the brachytherapy given alone, or with external beam radiotherapy? Was hormone therapy given, and for how long? What year was the treatment? Your oncology records will have all three.

Tell them

  • My right big toe has been dark and very sensitive to pressure for weeks, not minutes. Lead with this. It is what separates a Raynaud’s episode from continuously poor blood supply.
  • It has worsened steadily for months and has not levelled off. Primary Raynaud’s does not progress, so progression is itself a finding.
  • Both hands are affected too, during cycling. Easy to leave out as minor. It changes this from a leg problem to a possible whole-body one.
  • My mother had amputations caused by untreated vascular disease. A parent with limb-threatening artery disease raises the bar for being reassured.
  • Prostate cancer treated with brachytherapy — and whether external beam radiotherapy or hormone therapy were given too. Say all three parts. Seeds alone spare the leg arteries; an external beam through the pelvis does not, and hormone therapy raises cardiovascular risk.
  • I cycle 50–100 km a week in cleated shoes, with weight on my hands. Both a possible cause and something you can change. Doctors rarely ask.
  • I have never smoked, have no diabetes, normal blood pressure, and I am fit. Say it as a group. It is not reassurance, it is a finding: with the usual risk factors all absent, the cause is more likely to be autoimmune or small clots than ordinary furring of the arteries.
  • Here is my full list of medicines and supplements, including anything bought over the counter. Several common medicines narrow blood vessels and make Raynaud’s worse.

Ask them

  • Was my amlodipine prescribed for blood pressure or for the Raynaud’s, and can the dose be increased? 2.5 mg is a cautious starting dose, and this class of medicine is routinely increased for Raynaud’s when tolerated. If it has never been reviewed against symptoms, this is the fastest lever available.
  • Has secondary Raynaud’s been ruled out, and by which tests? The specific ones are nailfold capillaroscopy, ANA, anti-centromere, and anti-Scl-70. If none has been done, it has not been ruled out.
  • Can I have my ankle-brachial index and toe pressures measured, both legs? Ask for the toe reading explicitly. Ankle readings can look falsely normal when arteries are stiff, which hides disease in the small vessels.
  • Can I be referred to a rheumatologist as well as the vascular surgeon? New Raynaud’s at 68 in a man warrants the autoimmune workup whatever the arteries show. Two specialists, two different questions.
  • If the pulses in my foot are normal, should we still scan higher up for a source of small clots? This is the question that finds blue toe syndrome. It calls for a duplex ultrasound — a scan that shows both the shape of an artery and the blood moving through it — from the abdomen down, not the foot alone.
  • Can I have bloods done: full blood count, HbA1c, cholesterol, kidney function, ESR and CRP, and clotting? ESR and CRP are two general markers of inflammation. Together this panel covers diabetes, blood disorders, inflammation, and cardiovascular risk in one draw.
  • Are any of my current medicines making this worse? Beta blockers, decongestants, some migraine medicines, and stimulants all narrow blood vessels. Commonly overlooked.
  • Should I keep cycling, and at what intensity and temperature? Walking builds circulation and helps artery disease; cold-weather riding is a strong Raynaud’s trigger. Get a clear instruction rather than guessing.
  • Can I be referred to a podiatrist for the callus and nail care? Cutting or filing callus at home on a foot with poor blood supply risks a wound that will not heal.
  • What exactly should make me come back sooner, or go to A&E? Get their own list, in their words, written down before you leave.

What to do at home

With Raynaud’s confirmed, warmth is treatment rather than comfort. None of this replaces medical treatment and all of it is safe to start now.

Do

  • Warm your whole body, not just your feet. Blood is pulled away from the hands and feet when your trunk is cold, so a warm torso and a hat do more for your toes than thick socks alone.
  • Wear socks and warm shoes indoors, and warm the shoes before putting them on.
  • Dress for the cold before going out, not after feeling cold. Once an attack starts it is much harder to reverse.
  • When an attack starts: get somewhere warm, put your hands or feet in warm, not hot water, and move and massage them.
  • Check both feet daily in good light, including between and underneath the toes. Use a mirror.
  • Moisturise, but keep the skin between your toes dry so cracks do not let infection in.
  • Keep walking. Regular walking builds up small side-branch blood vessels and is the best-evidenced treatment for artery disease in the legs that does not involve surgery.
  • Manage stress. It triggers attacks through the same nerve pathway as cold. A real trigger, not a soft one.

Do not

  • No hot water bottles, heat pads, hot soaks, or radiators against your feet. Warm only. Skin with poor blood flow burns without the normal warning pain, and a burn there may not heal.
  • No tobacco, and avoid other people’s smoke.
  • No cutting, filing, or acid treatment of callus or corns at home. A podiatrist does this safely.
  • No decongestants containing pseudoephedrine, and check before starting anything new.
  • No compression socks or tight elastic unless a vascular specialist prescribes them. Compression on a foot already short of blood makes it worse.
  • No vibrating tools — angle grinders, hammer drills, mowers. Vibration worsens Raynaud’s and can cause it.
  • No aspirin or circulation supplements started on your own — but do ask the GP about low-dose aspirin this week, because it is a standard treatment if the cause turns out to be artery disease or small clots.
  • Cut back caffeine. It narrows blood vessels.

On the bike

  • Feet: winter overshoes, toe covers, and disposable chemical toe warmers. Air moving over your foot at speed cools it hard even in mild weather.
  • Shoes: wider toe box, better cushioning, looser straps. Move the cleats slightly rearward to take pressure off the ball of the foot. Never let a shoe squeeze a numb or discoloured toe.
  • Hands: thick, padded, windproof full-finger gloves. Change hand position every few minutes, and keep weight off the outer edge of the palm — that spot is where both the nerve and the artery are vulnerable.
  • Relax your grip, with slightly bent elbows, so your arms absorb road buzz instead of your hands. Padded bar tape and wider tyres at lower pressure help too.
  • Get your bike fit checked. Too much body weight carried on the hands is the root of most of these problems, and saddle and stem adjustments fix it.
  • Ease off for now. Ride the warmest part of the day, skip cold and wet rides, and stop any ride that brings on colour change or pain rather than pushing through.

A last word on the ranked list: the order reflects how well each cause fits your symptoms, not how likely it is. A condition placed first because missing it would be costly is not thereby the most probable one.
